FUNDA LOG · 05 / 07 from building a funda-question generator

Models know the headline and miss the hook

49%. That's how often a model could recall the one fact a quiz question turns on, with no search at all.

49%. That's how often glm-4.5-air could recall the one fact a quiz question turns on, with no search at all. Llama-3.3-70B got 47%.

A funda is built around a hook, the hidden fact you have to reach. The headline is the famous part everyone knows. To generate questions from articles I needed contexts that actually contained the hook, so the first thing to settle was whether a model could supply it from memory. I tested it against facts I'd verified with an independent web search:

approachrecall of the hook fact
glm-4.5-air, knowledge only49% (n=12)
llama-3.3-70b, knowledge only47% (n=12)
z.ai native web search, thinking on21% (n=6)
z.ai native web search, thinking off37% (n=6)
Google-backed search~92% (11 of 12 full chains)

Those are tiny samples, 12 and 6. Read them as a direction. The gap is big enough that the pipeline runs on the search.

The two LLMs failed on the same items, so a bigger model wasn't going to fix it. And they didn't fail quietly. I asked about Gopal Bhand, an 18th-century Bengali jester from Krishnanagar. z.ai answered "Kolhapur, Maharashtra, Marathi folklore." Groq answered "16th-century Bhakti poet from Mithila, Maithili." Different answers, both delivered with complete confidence.

Turning the model's thinking off made its search score higher and its data worse. With search failing, the no-thinking version fell back on what it half-remembered instead of saying it didn't know (it admitted the gap in 3 of 6 runs with thinking on, 0 of 6 with it off). And z.ai's own search index, asked about "Project Sunrise", returned a Japanese animation studio. I was after the Qantas flight project.

The thing that worked costs about $2: roughly 1,858 searches on Serper at a dollar per thousand.

Finding the article was half of it. The other half is finding the passage inside it, and that's where I spent the time. I tried three clever things.

Sentence embeddings: the same fact scored 0.82 cosine similarity against its paraphrase and different facts scored 0.79. Embedding models see topic, and every passage of an article is about the same topic. A cross-encoder trained for entailment: it scored a 500-character hook at a median of 0.01 and the same model gave 0.998 on a single claim, because entailment is atomic and the hook has many facts in it. Gzip distance: it couldn't tell padded trivia from real funda.

What beat all three was counting. Take the distinctive terms in the hook and measure what fraction of them show up in the passage. That moved source-finding from 0.25 to 0.60.

A decomposition helped on top. Splitting a question into three to six factual threads and searching each one separately found a source nothing else had: a blog post called an A to Z of Bengali fish. Fourteen URLs across all the threads, one of which served two of them. The threads really do need different sources.

Then I handed the whole job to a local 8B model. It writes a search query, reads the results, picks one, extracts the page, scores the match, and if the score is weak it tries again with a different angle, informed by what the last attempt returned. The retry is there for a measured reason. The fundas I couldn't source on the first try skewed 80% hard, against 51% for the bank overall, because hard fundas are built from material that doesn't surface on the first obvious search. One attempt biases the dataset toward the easy questions.

There's still a ceiling. About 19% of the bank's question-and-answer pairs use knowledge that no article I found contains.

All of this matters because of what the generator had been trained on. The training hooks were LLM summaries, and when I measured them, a summary held a median of 0.22 of the question's facts. The article it came from held 0.67. So the model learned to write a question from a context holding a fifth of what the question needed. The only way to imitate that is to make up the rest, and it did. That's a data defect, and I'd been looking at the model.

The fix is to cut the hook out of the article word for word. That's next. It hasn't run yet.
